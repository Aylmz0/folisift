## Introduction

The study asks whether a structured summary improves later recall compared with rereading the same text. The question matters because students often choose rereading even when retrieval practice is more effective. [SRC1, p. 3]

::: definition label="DEFINITION"
**Structured summary:** a short text that selects main ideas, deletes redundancy and preserves source claims. [SRC1, p. 3]
:::

## Methods

Participants read the same passage and were assigned to structured summary or rereading. Recall was measured after one week. The comparison isolates the study task, not the reading time. [SRC1, p. 4]

::: table caption="Study conditions" source="SRC1, p. 4" widths="34,33,33"
| Condition | Task | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Summary | Select and organize | Delayed recall |
| Rereading | Read again | Delayed recall |
:::

## Results

The summary condition recalled more main ideas than the rereading condition. The result is consistent with retrieval and organization accounts, but the sample limits generalization. [SRC1, p. 5]

::: critical label="INTERPRETATION"
The result does not prove that summaries replace practice testing. It supports structured summary as a comprehension task, followed by retrieval. [SRC1, p. 5]
:::

## Discussion

Use summaries to organize understanding, then test retrieval separately. Report the sample, the delay and the outcome measure; do not turn a small effect into a universal rule. [SRC1, p. 6]
